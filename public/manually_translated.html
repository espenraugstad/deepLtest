To say that the purpose of colleges is to provide instruction is like saying that General Motors' business is to operate assembly lines or that the purpose of medical care is to fill hospital beds.

Barr & Tagg, 1995, p.13

Student-centred approaches to teaching and learning are grounded in a constructivist perspective, according to which learning is understood as a continuous process of knowledge construction. The constructivist perspective is complemented by the sociocultural perspective, which emphasises that learning takes place through interaction with others. Together, these two perspectives provide the foundation for a paradigm shift from instruction to learning—that is, from teacher-centred to student-centred approaches to teaching and learning.

 

The constructivist perspective on learning

The constructivist perspective emerged in the early twentieth century through the work of developmental psychologist Jean Piaget (1896-1980). Piaget sought to understand how knowledge is constructed and structured. He maintained that knowledge is constructed as individuals explore the external world and interpret new experiences in light of their previous experiences. Roar Pettersen (2005) describes constructivism as follows:

Constructivism generally refers to the idea that we acquire and establish personal knowledge and knowledge structures in our own ways, through experience and through encounters with learning materials, theories, examples, and so forth. This is a continuous process in which “new” constructions both depend on and are connected to “old” constructions (p. 76; translated from Norwegian).

In the literature, the constructivist perspective is frequently discussed in conjunction with the sociocultural perspective. Tenenbaum et al. (2001) argue that different learners have different experiences and that knowledge is constructed on the basis of these experiences through interaction and dialogue with others:

Finally, collaboration is brought into the teaching and learning environment as it is through sharing individual knowledge, ideas and values with others that personal meaning making can be socially validated. As, under constructivism, ‘…external reality is likely to be perceived differently by different learners…it is through social negotiation that common meaning is constructed’ (Tenenbaum m. fl., 2001, p. 89).

The sociocultural perspective is one of the most influential perspectives on learning in education, although there is no single, universally accepted interpretation of it. During the early twentieth century, several philosophers developed different theories associated with this perspective, including the Americans John Dewey (1859-1952) and George Herbert Mead (1863-1931), and the Russians Lev Vygotsky (1896-1934) and Mikhail Bakhtin (1895-1975). Today, the sociocultural perspective may be understood as the view that learning takes place through social interaction with others. Olga Dysthe (2001) writes that “knowledge is constructed through interaction and within a context, rather than primarily through individual processes” (p. 42; translated from Norwegian). This understanding also reflects the constructivist perspective described above.

Roar Pettersen (2005) relates the sociocultural perspective directly to teaching: “Within a sociocultural frame of reference, the learner - whether a student or pupil - may be compared to a “researcher” who actively participates in a research community. The focus is on learning, understanding, and the acquisition of knowledge in collaboration with others” (p. 72; translated from Norwegian).

The paradigm shift from instruction to learning is one of the most important outcomes of these two perspectives. By providing student-centred rather than teacher-centred approaches to teaching and learning, instructors enable students to engage directly with the subject matter and construct new knowledge on the basis of knowledge they have previously acquired. Through learning activities that promote interaction and collaboration - for example, project-based learning - students can externalise and process their experiences. This creates a dynamic interplay between tacit and explicit knowledge.

 

From instruction to learning

Robert B. Barr and John Tagg examined the development of perspectives on teaching and learning in higher education over several decades. They concluded that although numerous attempts had been made to reform the education system and address its challenges, these efforts had largely remained within what they termed the ‘Instruction Paradigm’. Barr and Tagg (1995) illustrate this as follows:

For example, if students are not learning to solve problems or think critically, the old logic says we must teach a class in thinking and make it a general education requirement. The logic is all too circular: What students are learning in the classroom doesn't address their needs or ours; therefore, we must bring them back into another classroom and instruct them some more. (Barr & Tagg, 1995, s.14)

John Biggs: What the student does

Finally, we would like to introduce one of the most influential theorists in university pedagogy: the Australian professor John Biggs. Biggs (1999) analysed a range of teaching and learning situations and developed a model based on three fundamental conceptions of learning:

  1. Learning is primarily a direct result of individual differences between students.
  2. Learning is primarily the result of appropriate teaching.
  3. Learning results from learning-focused activities undertaken by students, shaped both by what they notice and take in and by the overall teaching context.

(Biggs, 1999, p. 61)

What does this mean for course teachers?

The shift from a paradigm centred on instruction to one centred on learning begins when instructors reflect on their own understanding of learning. A constructivist perspective emphasises the value of student-centred approaches to teaching and learning.

However, the transition from teacher-centred to student-centred teaching is not always straightforward, as Tynjala (1999) points out: "Designing constructivist learning environments requires of the teacher much more than traditional teaching because the emphasis shifts from the presentation of the information to guiding students' learning process" (p. 429). Shuell (1986) offers the following useful advice:

If students are to learn desired outcomes in a reasonably effective manner, then the teacher's fundamental task is to get students to engage in learning activities that are likely to result in their achieving those outcomes. ... It is helpful to remember that what the student does is actually more important in determining what is learned than what the teacher does. (Shuell, 1986, p. 429)

The following pages introduce a range of teaching approaches and principles that focus on student-centred learning. Not all of them involve educational technology, and some may be more appropriate for particular academic disciplines than others. Their shared aim, however, is to encourage students to participate actively and assume responsibility for their own learning.

Teaching practice does not need to be transformed radically overnight. We recommend beginning with small steps: try something new, consider how it works and whether it helps students achieve the intended learning outcomes more effectively, and then revise and expand upon what you have already begun
