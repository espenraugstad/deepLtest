To say that the purpose of colleges is to provide teaching is like saying that General Motors’ business is to run assembly lines or that the purpose of medical care is to fill hospital beds.

Barr & Tagg, 1995, p. 13

Student-centred teaching and learning approaches are based on a constructivist perspective on learning, in which learning is understood as a continuous process of knowledge construction. The constructivist learning perspective is complemented by the sociocultural learning perspective, in which learning takes place through interaction with others. These two learning perspectives form the basis for a paradigm shift from instruction to learning, i.e. from teacher-centred to student-centred teaching and learning methods.

A constructivist approach to learning

The constructivist approach to learning emerged in the early 20th century through the work of the developmental psychologist Jean Piaget (1896–1980). Piaget wanted to find out how knowledge is constructed and structured. He believed that knowledge is constructed by exploring the external world and processing new experiences in the light of previous experiences. Roar Pettersen describes constructivism as follows:

Constructivism generally refers to the way in which we acquire and establish personal knowledge and knowledge structures in our own way, through experience and through engagement with learning materials, theories, examples, etc. This is an ongoing process – in which ‘new’ constructions are both dependent on and linked to ‘old’ constructions (Pettersen, 2005, p. 76).

In the literature, we often find that the constructivist perspective on learning is mentioned in connection with the sociocultural perspective on learning. Tenenbaum et al. explain this by pointing out that different learners have different experiences and that it is through interaction and in conversations with other learners that knowledge is constructed on the basis of these experiences.

Finally, collaboration is integrated into the teaching and learning environment, as it is through sharing individual knowledge, ideas and values with others that personal meaning-making can be socially validated. As constructivism holds, ‘…external reality is likely to be perceived differently by different learners…it is through social negotiation that common meaning is constructed’ (Tenenbaum et al., 2001, p. 89).

The sociocultural perspective on learning is one of the most important perspectives in pedagogy, but there is no single, unified understanding of it. In the early 20th century, many philosophers developed various theories relating to this perspective, including the Americans John Dewey (1859–1952)  and George Herbert Mead (1863–1931) and the Russians Lev Vygotsky (1896–1934)  and Mikhail Bakhtin (1895–1975). Today, we can understand the sociocultural perspective on learning to mean that learning takes place through social interaction with others. Olga Dysthe writes that “knowledge is constructed through interaction and within a context, and not primarily through individual processes” (Dyste, 2001, p. 42). Here, too, we find the constructivist view of learning described above.

Roar Pettersen links the sociocultural perspective on learning very specifically to teaching: ‘Within a sociocultural frame of reference, the learner (students/pupils) can be likened to a “researcher” who actively participates in a research community. The focus is on learning, understanding and the acquisition of knowledge in collaboration with others’ (Pettersen, 2005, p. 72).

The paradigm shift from instruction to learning is one of the most important outcomes of these two learning perspectives. By offering student-centred rather than teacher-centred teaching and learning methods, students can gain their own experiences of the subject matter and build new knowledge on top of what they have previously learnt. Through learning activities that support interaction and collaboration – for example, in project-based learning – students can externalise and process their experiences. This leads to an interplay between tacit and explicit knowledge.

From instruction to learning

Robert B. Barr and John Tagg examined the development of teaching and learning perspectives in higher education over recent decades. They concluded that, although there have been many attempts to reform the education system and to address the challenges of the current system, these have taken place within what is known as ‘the Instruction Paradigm’. Barr & Tagg illustrate this as follows:

For example, if students are not learning to solve problems or think critically, the old logic suggests that we must teach a course in thinking and make it a general education requirement. The logic is all too circular: what students are learning in the classroom does not meet their needs or ours; therefore, we must bring them back into another classroom and teach them some more. John Biggs: What the student does

Finally, we would like to mention one of the most prominent theorists in university pedagogy, the Australian professor John Biggs. Biggs analysed various teaching and learning situations and developed a learning model based on three fundamental concepts of learning:

  1. Learning is primarily a direct result of individual differences between students.
  2. Learning is primarily the result of effective teaching.
  3. Learning is the result of learning-focused activities carried out by students, arising from both their observations and input and the overall teaching context.

(Biggs, 1999, p. 61, my translation)

Based on these three perspectives, we can define three levels of teaching competence.

There are two articles by Biggs in particular that may be worth knowing about: What the Student Does: teaching for enhanced learning (Biggs, 1999)  and Enhancing teaching through constructive alignment (Biggs, 1996).

In 2009, Aarhus University produced three videos that place Biggs’ model in the context of ‘Constructive Alignment’. The short film series *Teaching teaching and understanding understanding* has won numerous awards and consists of Video 1 – Learning: the student’s perspective, Video 2 – Understanding: the knowledge perspective  and Video 3 – The Solution: Constructive Alignment.

What does this mean for the subject teacher?

The shift from a paradigm centred on instruction to one centred on learning begins with subject teachers reflecting on their own perspective on learning. A constructivist approach to learning will focus on the value of student-centred teaching and learning methods.

The transition from teacher-centred to student-centred teaching is, however, not always that easy, as Tynjala (1999) confirms: “Designing constructivist learning environments requires much more of the teacher than traditional teaching because the emphasis shifts from the presentation of information to guiding students’ learning process” (Tynjala, 1999, p. 429). Shuell (1986) offers some good advice on this:

If students are to achieve the desired learning outcomes in a reasonably effective manner, then the teacher’s fundamental task is to encourage students to engage in learning activities that are likely to lead to their achieving those outcomes. ... It is worth remembering that what the student does is actually more important in determining what is learnt than what the teacher does. (Shuell, 1986, p. 429)

On the following pages, we will present various teaching methods and principles, all of which focus on student-centred learning. Not all of them will involve the use of teaching technology, and some may be better suited to certain subject areas than others, but the idea is that students should be active and take responsibility for their own learning. There is no need to make radical changes to your teaching from one day to the next. We recommend that you start with small steps – try something out, see how it works and whether it helps students achieve their learning objectives more effectively, and then revise and expand on what you have already started.
